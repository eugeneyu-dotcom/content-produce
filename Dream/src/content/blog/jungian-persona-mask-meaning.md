## The Game Everyone Says Is the Best JRPG Ever Made

<p>Have you played Persona 5 Royal? There's a saying that gets thrown around in gaming circles constantly: within JRPGs, P5R is untouchable. It sounds like an exaggeration until you've actually played it, and then it stops sounding like one.</p><p>One of the game's more interesting mechanics is the <a href="/category/jungian-archetypes/">Jungian archetypes</a> baked directly into combat: each character wields a Persona, a mask-like entity with its own powers, summoned mid-fight to attack.</p>

## The Mask Concept Isn't Fiction — It's Jung

<p>That persona mechanic isn't something the writers invented from scratch. It's lifted straight from Carl Jung's own psychology. Jung argued that the persona is the social face an individual builds to meet the expectations of the people around them — a tool for making a specific impression, and simultaneously a layer that hides whatever's underneath it.</p>

## An Old Theory That Refuses to Stop Being Useful

<p>Jungian psychology is, by most accounts, an older theory in the field, and its scientific standing gets questioned fairly often. But as a reference point for games, or as something to bring up over drinks, both Freud's and Jung's ideas keep getting attention precisely because they're dramatic and make for a good story. P5R took that theory and turned it into something genuinely entertaining for a mass audience — next time you're playing, it's worth actually looking at what each character's mask design is doing.</p>

## The Mask Has an Opposite, and It's Not On Your Side

<p>The game's combat encounters, called Shadows, come from the same source — they're the persona's opposite number. In Jungian terms, the mask and the <a href="/blog/jungian-shadow-dreams/">Shadow</a> exist in a light-and-dark, mutually dependent relationship: the more flawless your mask gets, the stronger your Shadow grows underneath it.</p><table><tr><th></th><th>Persona (the mask)</th><th>Shadow</th></tr><tr><td>Function</td><td>Presented to the world, built for approval</td><td>Repressed, kept out of view</td></tr><tr><td>Grows stronger when</td><td>Refined, perfected, worn constantly</td><td>The mask above it gets more perfect</td></tr><tr><td>Shows up as</td><td>Your public personality</td><td>What you deny about yourself</td></tr></table>

## Why That One Person Bothers You So Much

<p>There's a specific mechanism worth knowing here: if you find yourself unusually irritated by a certain type of person, that might be projection doing its work. Your persona doesn't permit you to become that type, so the dislike shows up loud and clear on the surface — while underneath, there may be a buried desire to actually be that type of person. <a href="https://www.thecollector.com/jungian-persona-what-are-the-masks-we-wear/">Jungian writers</a> describe this dynamic often, and once you notice it in yourself once, it's hard to stop noticing it.</p>

## The Rest of Jung's Cast

<p>None of this makes the theory scientifically airtight — plenty of modern psychology has moved past strict Jungian frameworks. But as a lens for understanding why certain people get under your skin, or why a video game character's mask is doing more narrative work than it looks like, it holds up better than it has any right to. If the persona/shadow pairing interests you, the <a href="/blog/anima-and-animus-in-dreams/">anima and animus</a> are the other half of Jung's core cast worth knowing.</p>
