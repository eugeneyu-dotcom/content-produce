## Taipei Is One of the Safest Cities on Earth. My Dreams Didn't Get the Memo.

<p>A while back, for reasons that don't matter much here, I ended up living in Taipei for a stretch. Taipei consistently ranks as one of the safest cities in the world by most global indexes, which on paper meant my day-to-day risk of encountering real danger had dropped about as low as it gets. And yet, during exactly that period, I kept having the same dream over and over: being hunted down by someone. Which made no sense on the surface, since I've never actually been chased by anyone in my life — there was no real incident this could plausibly be a memory of. This is the kind of gap that <a href="/category/evolutionary-nightmares/">evolutionary nightmare research</a> exists to explain.</p>

## The Assumption That Every Nightmare Needs a Real-Life Villain

<p>The default explanation most people reach for is that a nightmare must be mirroring some specific anxiety or trauma from waking life — as if every bad dream has to correspond to one identifiable bad thing that actually happened. But a lot of the time, what we're really doing is dreaming about danger that never occurred at all. That could still be some abstract anxiety wearing a costume, sure. But I've come to think there's a cleaner explanation for why this particular pattern shows up regardless of how safe your actual life is: threat simulation theory.</p>

## A Theory With a Name, Not Just a Vibe

<p>Finnish cognitive scientist <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antti_Revonsuo">Antti Revonsuo</a> proposed the threat simulation theory in 2000, arguing that the evolutionary function of dreaming — nightmares especially — is for the brain to rehearse encountering danger and practicing escape or defense, all from the safety of sleep. You don't need to have lived through the actual threat yourself. The brain already comes pre-loaded with a rehearsal script for being pursued by something dangerous, because that's exactly the kind of threat our ancestors faced constantly and repeatedly, long before any of us were born to inherit the wiring. A <a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15766897/">2005 review</a> of the theory backs this up directly, describing dream threat simulation as rehearsing the cognitive mechanisms needed for efficient threat perception and avoidance.</p>

## Why This Beats the Alternatives, For Me

<p>I find this a lot more convincing than dreams being disguised wishes, or just random neural firing with no function at all, mostly because it actually explains why being chased, falling, and drowning show up as dream themes across so many <a href="/blog/chased-dreams-psychology-meaning/">cultures and generations</a> without needing a personal trauma behind each instance. It also explains why the script keeps updating rather than staying frozen. The threat used to be a predator in a literal jungle; now it's an unfamiliar stranger with bad intent in a concrete one. None of that is really about individual psychological damage — it's more like a rehearsal drill baked into the species' memory, and the drill just gets re-skinned depending on what danger modern humans are actually more likely to run into.</p>

## Where the Theory Gets Tested Against Real Danger

<p>Revonsuo's theory eventually got checked against people actually living under real threat, not just safe city-dwellers having odd dreams. Researchers found that children growing up in war zones and other genuinely dangerous conditions had nightmares containing more frequent and more intense danger content than children living somewhere calmer — which lines up with a <a href="https://humanities.uct.ac.za/sites/default/files/content_migration/humanities_uct_ac_za/1233/files/Sheri.Koopowitz.pdf">comparative study</a> on exactly this question. That's a reasonably strong signal that this isn't just a nice-sounding idea — the rehearsal function seems to actually respond to real environmental danger, scaling up or down with it.</p>

## The Rehearsal Doesn't Care Whether the Threat Ever Shows Up

<p>Which brings me back to Taipei, and to a dream that, statistically, had almost nothing to draw on. The adaptive function of a bad dream (more on that below) doesn't require the danger to have ever been real for you personally — the rehearsal script runs regardless, on a schedule dictated by something much older than your own biography. Living somewhere with a near-zero crime rate didn't turn off the simulation. It just meant the simulation had nothing local to attach itself to, so it ran the generic version instead.</p>

## The Other Explanation I've Argued For: Stress

<p>I should be fair to the opposing view, because I've argued it myself. In an earlier piece about a stretch of recurring chase dreams, I came down on the other side. The scenes kept changing: sometimes I was chased by wild beasts in a jungle, sometimes by an unknown creature in a maze, and the funniest time was when I was chased by Hisoka from the anime 'Hunter x Hunter'. The dream never had an ending and I wouldn't actually die, but it hurt my sleep quality. Waking up wasn't restful; it was more like being pulled out of a low-grade alarm state, leaving me groggy and on edge for hours.</p><p>Back then I read threat simulation as a defense mechanism evolved by our ancestors, and I didn't buy it as the whole story. In relatively safe modern society you rarely get chased, yet such dreams still persist, and I leaned toward a different explanation: dreaming of being chased is because your current life stress is too high. That matched my own experience. When I was traveling, I didn't have any dreams of being chased. My mind was clear, my responsibilities minimal, and my sleep was genuinely restorative.</p><p>The brain is involved either way. The emotional centers, especially the <a href="/blog/dreams-brain-emotion-sleep/">amygdala</a>, are highly active during REM sleep, the stage where most intense dreaming occurs, and that region handles fear and threat processing, so intensely real scenarios are no surprise. <a href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1053810005000498">Research</a> shows distinct neural activity patterns here, and <a href="https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11515147/">work on bad dreams</a> offers several readings of what they are for. Some <a href="https://strongerminds.co.uk/understanding-nightmares-causes-evolutionary-role-and-interventions/">mental health professionals</a> already treat daily stress as a considerable factor.</p><table><thead><tr><th>Theory</th><th>Primary mechanism</th><th>Relevance to modern life</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>Threat simulation</td><td>Prepares the brain for real-world dangers by rehearsing fight-or-flight scenarios.</td><td>Less direct for physical threats; perhaps mental "threats" like public speaking.</td></tr><tr><td>Stress / emotion processing</td><td>The brain processes unresolved daytime anxieties, trauma, or emotional overload.</td><td>Highly relevant; modern stressors are predominantly psychological.</td></tr><tr><td>Random neural activity</td><td>Dreams are a byproduct of neural cleanup or memory consolidation, without inherent meaning.</td><td>A more passive, less adaptive function for nightmares.</td></tr></tbody></table><p>The contrast isn't always clear-cut, and both the rehearsal account and the stress account could contribute to why these dreams persist. If stress is a major driver, the modern version of the evolutionary story shifts: our threats aren't typically physical predators but deadlines, financial worries, relationship conflicts and the relentless hum of digital information, and the brain translates that pressure into the primal imagery of being chased or trapped. In that case the first question to ask after a chase dream might be 'What is genuinely stressing me out right now?'</p>

## Occasional Bad Dreams Aren't Trauma Nightmares, and May Even Be Healthy

<p>One distinction matters here. I'm not talking about nightmares that stem from PTSD, which are usually highly repetitive and accompanied by intense suffering, but about occasional, random, and absurd ones. The plane crash and the being-chased dreams I've mentioned before both fall into that group. It's like the difference between tripping over a curb and falling down a flight of stairs: both are disruptive, but one carries a far greater, lasting impact.</p><p>For these occasional nightmares, I've come to think they may actually be healthy. Besides the threat simulation theory above, another account says the brain rearranges emotional memories during REM sleep. Through nightmares it turns abstract waking anxiety and fear into concrete scenarios, similar to 'exposure therapy' in psychology. That can help desensitize the nervous system and reduce anxiety when awake, a healthy emotional 'detoxification' process, in the same way <a href="/blog/rem-sleep-and-memory-consolidation-study/">sleep consolidates</a> new memories. For related theories, you can refer to <a href="https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-023-43319-z">this paper</a>. Psychology is a rapidly developing field, though, and new evidence or theories may emerge at any time to reshape this picture.</p>
