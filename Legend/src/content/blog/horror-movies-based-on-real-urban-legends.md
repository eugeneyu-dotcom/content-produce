## "Based on a True Story" Usually Means Something Narrower Than It Sounds

<p>Horror marketing loves the phrase "based on true events," but most of the time that means one specific documented crime or haunting case got adapted for the screen. The five films in this entry from <a href="/category/legends-on-screen/">Legends on Screen</a> are a different category entirely: each one adapts a genuine urban legend — a story that was already being told, warned about, or ritually performed by ordinary people before any studio got involved. The legend came first. The movie just gave it a bigger audience.</p>

## The Mothman Prophecies (2002)

<p>The film dramatizes journalist John Keel's investigation into sightings of a tall, winged, red-eyed creature reported around Point Pleasant, West Virginia between November 1966 and December 1967 — sightings that locals connected, after the fact, to the collapse of the Silver Bridge. Whatever the creature actually was, the reports themselves are well documented, and the article covering the <a href="/blog/historical-evidence-of-the-mothman/">historical evidence behind the Mothman sightings</a> goes through the primary sources in more detail than the film has time for. It's also worth reading how <a href="/blog/cryptids-vs-urban-legends-differences-mothman/">Mothman differs from a typical urban legend</a> — the sighting reports predate any campfire retelling.</p>

## Candyman (1992)

<p>The ritual at the center of the film — say a name in a mirror a set number of times and the killer appears — is a direct cousin of the mirror-summoning games covered in this site's <a href="/category/bloody-mary-ritual-legend/">Bloody Mary ritual coverage</a>. Director Bernard Rose leaned on Clive Barker's short story but layered in real Chicago urban legend research, which is part of why the film's premise felt immediately familiar to audiences who'd grown up daring each other to say a name into a bathroom mirror. The "summon by ritual" structure is one of the most durable patterns in this genre, showing up again and again under different names.</p>

## A Nightmare on Elm Street (1984)

<p>Wes Craven has said publicly that the idea came from a series of Los Angeles Times articles about Hmong refugees, several of whom died in their sleep shortly after reporting recurring nightmares severe enough that they were afraid to fall asleep again. Doctors at the time had no clean explanation for the deaths. Craven kept the core unsettling logic — a nightmare that can follow you out of sleep and kill you — and built Freddy Krueger around it. It's one of the rare cases where the "urban legend" being adapted wasn't folklore at all, but an unexplained real-world pattern that folklore-shaped itself around.</p>

## Slender Man (2018)

<p>By the time this film reached theaters, the character had already gone through the full life cycle this section of the site tracks closely — forum origin, amateur mythology-building, and the video game that actually made it a household name, covered step by step in <a href="/blog/slender-man-video-game-viral-origin/">how Slender Man went viral through a game, not the story</a>. The 2018 film arrived after the character's darkest real-world chapter, the 2014 Wisconsin stabbing, was already a matter of public record — which the movie mostly sidesteps, choosing to adapt the digital-folklore aesthetic rather than the crime itself.</p>

## Ouija (2014)

<p>Spirit boards carry decades of folk warnings that have nothing to do with Hollywood: don't play alone, always say goodbye before ending a session, never let the planchette stop on the board mid-message. The film borrows that existing etiquette-as-horror structure wholesale. The <a href="/blog/history-of-spirit-summoning-games/">history of spirit-summoning games</a> on this site traces where those specific rules actually came from, and it turns out most of them are considerably older than any Ouija-branded film franchise.</p>

## Why This List Will Probably Keep Growing

<p>Urban legends are, by definition, stories that were already spreading person-to-person before anyone tried to sell tickets to them — which means the supply of adaptable material never really runs out. As long as new legends keep circulating online the way Slender Man did, more of them will eventually get the film treatment. This entry will get updated as that keeps happening.</p>
