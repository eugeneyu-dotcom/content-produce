## The rumor almost everyone has heard

<p>Plenty of people have heard that somewhere out there are Satan-worshipping cults, running strange rituals — and that the ritual you hear about most often is the abuse and sacrifice of children. It's one of the stickiest stories of the last half-century, and it belongs squarely in the study of <a href="/category/moral-panic-urban-legends/">moral panics</a>.</p><p>What makes it worth revisiting isn't the horror of the claim. It's how a fear this enormous, this specific, and this destructive was assembled almost from scratch — and how ordinary the ingredients turned out to be.</p>

## One book, one starting point

<p>The whole thing seems to trace back to the 1980s, and to the publication of a book called Michelle Remembers. Before it, ritual-abuse stories drifted at the edges of folklore. After it, they had a shape, a vocabulary, and a bestseller's reach. <a href="https://www.history.com/articles/what-was-satanic-panic-1980s">History.com</a> lays out how quickly that single text moved from a publisher's list into talk shows, sermons, and eventually police interview rooms.</p><p>A story doesn't need to be true to be useful. It needs to be repeatable — and this one was built to be repeated.</p>

## Why so many people believed it

<p>That book told the story of a Canadian woman who, through hypnosis, recovered memories of being abused by a satanic cult as a child — vivid, detailed stuff. In an era when people didn't really understand hypnosis, many took it at face value, and a wave of daycare and preschool cases alleging satanic ritual abuse erupted.</p><p>The mechanism was the same one that powers most panics: an authority figure, a frightening claim, and an audience with no reason to doubt the method. If you've read my breakdown of <a href="/blog/white-van-abduction-rumors-social-media/">white van rumors</a>, the pattern will feel familiar — the details change, the machinery doesn't.</p>

## The memories were real. The events weren't.

<p>But later, as investigations went on and our understanding of hypnosis improved, it turned out many of these were false memories implanted during hypnosis — extremely common in that period, where people would create a memory out of nothing because of a hypnotist's unconscious suggestion. This is the part that unsettles me most. The people recounting these horrors often weren't lying. They genuinely remembered things that had never happened.</p><p>That's the quiet danger sitting under the whole affair: memory isn't a recording you play back, it's a story you rebuild each time, and a confident question can rewrite it.</p>

## McMartin: the case that broke the spell

<p>The most famous example, the McMartin Preschool Trial, was later shown to have no supporting evidence, and the therapists had repeatedly used leading questions during interviews. Cases like these pushed people to take interview technique and therapeutic standards far more seriously — especially when questioning children, whose cognitive faculties aren't fully developed and who find it harder to separate real memories from imagined ones.</p><table><tr><th>The claim</th><th>What investigation found</th></tr><tr><td>Organized satanic abuse in daycares</td><td>No physical or corroborating evidence</td></tr><tr><td>Children's detailed testimony</td><td>Shaped by repeated leading questions</td></tr><tr><td>Hypnotically recovered memories</td><td>Consistent with suggestion, not recall</td></tr></table><p>An academic <a href="https://digitalcommons.memphis.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=4629&context=etd">analysis of the panic</a> traces exactly how interview practice, not evidence, produced the accounts.</p>

## What the panic actually left behind

<p>The lasting legacy isn't a network of cults. It's a set of hard lessons about how we ask questions, and of whom. The <a href="https://cei.org/blog/the-origins-and-lessons-of-satanic-panic-of-the-1980s/">origins and lessons</a> of the episode read less like a horror story and more like a manual for how good intentions, bad method, and a captivated public can wreck real lives — the accused adults, and the children coached into believing their own invented nightmares.</p><p>It's the same reason I keep coming back to stories like the <a href="/blog/organ-harvesting-legend-debunked-fact-fear/">organ harvesting legend</a>. The scariest thing in these tales was never the devil. It was us — 43 million television viewers, a handful of certain-sounding experts, and a memory that bends toward whatever it's asked to remember.</p>
