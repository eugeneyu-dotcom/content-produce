## The Girl in the Bobbed Haircut

<p>Hanako-san is a Japanese school legend about the ghost of a girl who haunts a toilet, and she has been rewritten so often that her image now differs from film to film. If you watch much Japanese film or anime, you have probably seen her without knowing the name: a small girl with a bobbed haircut, a white shirt and a red suspender skirt, standing where a girl should never be standing.</p>
<p>That image comes from the legend known as Toire no Hanako-san, or Hanako of the Toilet. She belongs with the other <a href="/category/bloody-mary-ritual-legend/">school-ritual legends</a> that spread among children by word of mouth, the same family as <a href="/blog/kokkuri-san-how-to-play/">Kokkuri-san</a> and <a href="/blog/daruma-san-bath-game-ritual/">Daruma-san</a>.

## How the Ritual Goes

<p>The rules change from school to school, but the common core is short:</p>
<ul>
<li>Go to a girls' toilet, usually the one on the third floor.</li>
<li>Knock three times on the third stall.</li>
<li>Ask whether Hanako-san is there.</li>
<li>She is said to answer that she is, in words that vary by version.</li>
</ul>
<p>What happens next depends on who is telling it. In some versions a bloody or ghostly hand appears. In others, she pulls the person into the toilet, in some tellings all the way to hell. One version swaps her for a three-headed lizard that eats you for invading her privacy, which tells you how freely the story gets rewritten. <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hanako-san">Wikipedia</a> lists these variants side by side.

## Nobody Agrees Where She Came From

<p>The folklorist Matthew Meyer dates the legend to the 1950s, and a 2014 NPR article quoted on the same page calls her a fixture of Japanese urban folklore over roughly the last seventy years. Beyond that, her origin is a matter of competing stories.</p>
<p>The most common story ties her to school bullying, with a girl who died by suicide in a school toilet. Others make her a victim attacked and killed at school, by an attacker or an abusive parent, and one has her as a child who died in a wartime air raid while hiding in a toilet during a game of hide-and-seek. Nothing in these versions can be checked, which is part of why the legend travels so easily.

## Four Hanakos, Across Film and Manga

<p>Compared with an ordinary ghost story, this legend has a surprisingly warm side. In the first version to reach the screen, the 1995 film Toire no Hanako-san, directed by Joji Matsuoka, Hanako is a gentle, protective spirit who helps students being bullied.</p>
<p>That did not last. The 1998 film Shinsei Toire no Hanako-san, directed by Yukihiko Tsutsumi, turns her into a vengeful spirit, one that blends in the legend of the cursed doll. The character keeps changing, and the table shows how far.</p>

<p>The manga Toilet-Bound Hanako-kun, by AidaIro, debuted in 2014, and the Lerche anime premiered in early 2020. In it, Hanako is a scheming but reliable boy ghost, in charge of keeping the school's various legends in balance. The girl in the red skirt turned into a boy, and the character still works.

## Why She Keeps Being Rewritten

<p>I think that is the real reason Hanako-san has lasted: nobody owns the details. A legend whose rules can be changed from one school to the next can be changed again in a film, and again in a manga, without anyone feeling cheated. The same flexibility shows up in <a href="/blog/kuchisake-onna-origin-story-explained/">Kuchisake-onna</a>, whose origin stories also contradict each other.</p>
<p>If you want to know which Hanako is the real one, the honest answer is that none of them are, and that every one of them is. The one you heard in your school toilet is simply the one that reached you.
