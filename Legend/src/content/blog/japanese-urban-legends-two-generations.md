## Why These Stories Suddenly Feel Familiar

<p>A Japanese urban legend, or <em>toshi densetsu</em>, is a story that circulates as true, and most of them involve something paranormal, as <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_urban_legends" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Wikipedia</a> puts it. They have never been easier to run into outside Japan, because films and games keep carrying them abroad. It is a large part of what I think of as <a href="/category/cross-cultural-urban-legends/">cross-cultural folklore</a>: the same few fears showing up in different costumes.</p>
<p>I've written here before about <a href="/blog/kuchisake-onna-origin-story-explained/">Kuchisake-onna</a> and <a href="/blog/hanako-san-toilet-ghost-legend/">Hanako-san</a>, and as far as I can tell most people meet those two through a movie before they hear them from anyone. My own favorite is a different one: Hasshaku-sama, a woman taller than 2.4 meters who is always described in a white dress and a wide-brimmed white hat.</p>
<p>When I lined all of these up, they sorted themselves into two generations, and the difference between them says more than any single legend does.</p>

## Generation One: Stories Passed From Child to Child

<p>The older group has no author anyone can name. These legends moved by word of mouth, mostly among schoolchildren, and they are tied to a place and a rule: a toilet you knock on, a game you must not finish, a question you must answer correctly.</p>

<p>Because nobody owns them, these stories keep changing as they pass from one group of children to the next, which is exactly how Hanako-san ended up with so many faces.</p>

## Generation Two: Stories With a Timestamp

<p>The newer group starts from a single post. <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/5channel" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">2channel</a> is an anonymous Japanese textboard founded in 1999 by Hiroyuki Nishimura, where posting is anonymous and the name field is seldom used. Several well-known modern legends trace back to it. Kisaragi Station is one: it appeared there in 2004, as an account by a poster stranded at a station that appears on no map.</p>
<p>The elevator game is the awkward case. The version most often cited was posted on 2channel around 2008, but Korea is where the game spread most widely, and the origin is disputed. I won't repeat the ritual here, because the <a href="/blog/elevator-game-ritual/">elevator game</a> article already goes through it floor by floor. It is a useful reminder that even a stories-with-a-timestamp legend can have an origin people argue about.</p>
<p>Beyond Hasshaku-sama, Kisaragi Station and the elevator game, I think of 2channel as the birthplace of modern weird stories in Japan. Those three can be traced back to it, or to stories very like it.</p>

## The One I Keep Coming Back To: Hasshaku-sama

<p>I first met this legend without knowing it, through Lady Dimitrescu, the tall vampire in Resident Evil Village. I was playing the game and became fascinated with her. Most monsters need something to frighten you: a face that looks wrong, or features that aren't human at all. She needs only her height and her presence. She also holds her own beside the game's other bosses, and that is the reason I rate the character design so highly.</p>
<p>The game version differs from the original in a few ways. In the original, Hasshaku-sama often gives a strange laugh and imitates the voice of the victim's relatives to lure them out. In the game, Lady Dimitrescu is gentler at first and never uses a man's voice.</p>
<p>The details of the first written version are on <a href="https://yokai.jp/en/yokai/hachishakusama" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">YOKAI.JP</a>. It was posted on 26 August 2008 in a thread on 2channel's occult board, as replies 908 to 916 from a single poster. The figure is about eight shaku tall, roughly 2.42 meters. The narrator sees a hat moving above a hedge about two meters high, then a woman in a whitish dress. Later, a voice at the window sounds almost exactly like his grandfather's, asking if he is all right. His grandfather tells him afterward that he never called.</p>
<p>What fewer people know is that this legend is young. It has circulated only since August 2008, which makes it much newer than Hanako-san or Kuchisake-onna. As for the game, <a href="https://the-line-up.com/lady-dimitrescu" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">The Line-Up</a> reports that Capcom's art director, Tomonori Takano, named Hasshaku-sama as one of three influences on Lady Dimitrescu, alongside Elizabeth Bathory and Morticia Addams, and the same piece notes that she may not be an exact analogue.</p>

## A Few Others You'll Run Into

<p>The two groups above are not the whole map. Wikipedia lists dozens more, and a handful come up constantly:</p>
<table><tr><th>Legend</th><th>How Wikipedia describes it</th></tr>
<tr><td>Teke Teke</td><td>A legless vengeful spirit who slices victims in half</td></tr>
<tr><td>Aka Manto</td><td>A bathroom spirit who makes you choose red or blue paper, with deadly consequences</td></tr>
<tr><td>Kunekune</td><td>A white shimmering figure seen in distant fields that drives viewers mad</td></tr>
<tr><td>Red Room Curse</td><td>A pop-up ad that foretells the viewer's death</td></tr>
<tr><td>Jinmenken</td><td>Dogs with human faces that can outrun cars</td></tr></table>
<p>The same page treats the Red Room Curse as an early Japanese internet urban legend, and says early reports of Kunekune appeared on several websites at once. Jinmenken is the reverse: it spread across Japan in 1989, though rumors may go back to the 1950s. So the line between the generations is not tidy, and I'd rather show that than pretend it is.</p>

## What Changes When a Story Has a Date

<p>This part is my own reading, not something I can point to a source for. The older legends can't be dated, so their owners are everybody and nobody: each retelling can change the rules, which is why a toilet ghost can be a protector in one film and a vengeful spirit in the next. A 2channel story starts as a fixed text. You can point to the thread, the reply numbers and the minute it was posted, and the arguments turn to who wrote it rather than who first heard it.</p>
<p>It also changes where the story goes next. Hasshaku-sama reached a global audience through a video game, not a school corridor. And what I like most about her, a figure that frightens you simply by standing there, is the opposite of how the older legends work, which lean on a place and a rule.</p>
